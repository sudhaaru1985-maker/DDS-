Pathology asks WHY and HOW. It explores the “whys” and “wherefores” of disease: why does this patient have these signs and symptoms, and how did the disease develop?

The Greek roots of “pathology” literally mean…

Answer: B. Pathos = suffering; logos = study. Pathology studies the structural and functional changes in cells, tissues and organs that underlie disease.

Subdivisions of human pathology

General pathology

Common reactions of cells and tissues to injury: the principles apply to ANY organ

  • Cell injury, adaptation & death
  • Inflammation & repair
  • Haemodynamic disorders
  • Neoplasia
  • Immune & genetic disorders

Systemic pathology

Diseases of specific organs and body systems

  • Oral cavity, jaws & salivary glands
  • Cardiovascular & respiratory systems
  • Gastrointestinal tract & liver
  • Endocrine, renal, skin and more

Tip: general pathology is the GRAMMAR of disease; systemic pathology is the VOCABULARY.

Diagnostic pathology: tissues & fluids

Biopsy

Tissue removed from a living patient for DIAGNOSIS

Incisional (part of lesion) · Excisional (whole small lesion) · Needle core · Punch · Endoscopic

Resection specimen

All or part of an organ removed for an ALREADY-DIAGNOSED condition

e.g., partial glossectomy, mandibulectomy, parotidectomy

Body fluids

Fluids examined for cells, chemicals and microbes

Blood · Urine · Saliva · Pleural/ascitic fluid · CSF

Checkpoint 7 / 16SLIDO · MULTIPLE CHOICE

A patient with biopsy-proven carcinoma of the tongue has part of the tongue removed (partial glossectomy). This specimen is a…

Answer: C. The diagnosis was already made on biopsy, so the removed organ part is a RESECTION specimen. The pathologist now reports tumour size, depth of invasion, margins and lymph nodes (staging).

Cytology: studying dispersed cells

Exfoliative cytology

Cells shed, scraped or brushed off an epithelial surface

e.g., oral brush cytology of a white patch; Pap smear

Fluid cytology

Cells withdrawn in the fluid they are suspended in

e.g., pleural or ascitic fluid, urine, CSF

Washings

Cells flushed out of an organ with an irrigating fluid

e.g., bronchial / bronchoalveolar lavage

Fine-needle aspiration (FNAC)

Cells sucked out of a solid lump with a thin needle and syringe

e.g., salivary gland, neck lymph node, thyroid

Mainly used for cancer and pre-cancer: quick · cheap · minimally invasive

Video 2 · Active viewing

Fine-needle aspiration: smear preparation

👁 Watch for…

  1. How is the aspirated material placed on the slide?
  2. How is the smear spread without crushing the cells?
  3. Why are some slides air-dried and others put straight into alcohol?
  1. Bevel down, close to the slide; a small drop is expelled
  2. A second slide is drawn gently across it (no heavy pressure)
  3. Air-dried → Romanowsky/Diff-Quik stains; wet-fixed in alcohol → Papanicolaou (Pap) stain

Checkpoint 9 / 16SLIDO · MULTIPLE CHOICE

Which statement is TRUE?

Answer: B. Invasion is an architectural feature, so you need intact tissue (histology) to see it. Cytology can strongly suggest malignancy, but it cannot show invasion.

Techniques of pathology: the classic toolkit

Light microscopy & H&E

The workhorse. Haematoxylin stains nuclei blue-purple; eosin stains cytoplasm pink.

Histochemistry (special stains)

The chemistry of tissues. PAS → fungi (Candida) & glycogen · Congo red → amyloid · Perls’ → iron

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Labelled antibodies bind specific proteins (antigens) in tissue. Cytokeratin → carcinoma · CD45 → lymphoma · Ki-67 → proliferation

Electron microscopy

Organelle-level detail and virus particles; now used selectively (e.g., kidney biopsies)

Pathology today: modern techniques

Molecular pathology

PCR, in-situ hybridisation (ISH/FISH) and next-generation sequencing detect DNA/RNA changes.

e.g., HPV in oropharyngeal cancer; mutations that guide targeted therapy

Digital pathology & AI

Glass slides scanned into whole-slide images: view anywhere, share for second opinions.

AI assists detection & grading; the pathologist signs out

Liquid biopsy

Tumour DNA fragments (ctDNA) detected in a blood sample.

Saliva-based tests for oral cancer are being researched

Automated lab medicine

Analysers run biochemistry & haematology in minutes.

e.g., glucose/HbA1c, troponin, full blood count, PT/INR

Worked example: dental caries

Epidemiology

One of the most common diseases worldwide: about 2 billion people have caries of permanent teeth (WHO, 2022)

Aetiology

Cariogenic bacteria (Streptococcus mutans, lactobacilli) + fermentable sugars + susceptible tooth + time

Pathogenesis

Bacteria in plaque ferment sugars → acids → pH falls below ~5.5 → enamel demineralises

Morphology

Chalky white-spot lesion → brown discolouration → cavitation; softened dentine

Clinical features

Sensitivity to sweet or cold → toothache

Complications & prognosis

Pulpitis → pulp necrosis → periapical abscess, cellulitis. Early lesions can remineralise with fluoride

Checkpoint 13 / 16SLIDO · MULTIPLE CHOICE

A 22-year-old has swollen, bleeding gums that did not improve after scaling, plus tiredness and easy bruising. What is the FIRST investigation?

Answer: B. Gingival enlargement + bleeding + fatigue + bruising suggests acute leukaemia. Haematology (FBC and blood film) comes first, and early referral can be life-saving.
Checkpoint 14 / 16SLIDO · MULTIPLE CHOICE

A patient taking warfarin needs a tooth extraction tomorrow. Which laboratory result must you check?

Answer: B. INR measures the anticoagulant effect of warfarin. Check a recent INR (usually within 24–72 h) before extraction and follow local or national guidelines. This is pathology keeping your practice safe.
